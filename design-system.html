Functional Fusion Protocol

How these documents are built.

A working reference so anything added later reads as part of the same system. This is not a general style guide. It records the decisions actually made for the prescription pad and the formulary, and why, because the reasons are what keep the next piece cohesive when the situation is slightly different.

The rules that outrank everything

Start here. A layout that violates one of these is wrong even when it looks better, and most of the visual system exists to serve them.

  • It is never a menu.

    The NP has already had the clinical conversation. The patient reads a determination, not a set of options. Every layout decision serves this, and it is the one rule that outranks aesthetics. If a device makes the sheet feel selectable, it is wrong even if it looks better.

  • The foundation is not markable.

    Foundational Labs and Nutritional Foundation sit in a full-bleed blue band with their green dots printed already filled. Nothing about them is a field. The absence of anything to mark is the argument: it is already yours, decided before the sheet was printed.

  • Green ink means prescribed.

    Green appears nowhere decorative. A green dot on a pathway means the NP marked it. Because green never means anything else, a patient can read the sheet at a glance and see only what is theirs. Spending green on decoration would cost the sheet its clearest signal.

  • Blank means excluded, and says so.

    Unmarked pathways stay on the sheet but recede, and a printed line states that anything unmarked is not part of the protocol. Never leave an empty field ambiguous between “not prescribed” and “forgotten”.

  • Vocabulary is clinical, not retail.

    Prescribed, marked by your NP, determined from your labs, return for reassessment. Never select, choose, options, packages or available on a clinical document.

  • Every protocol has an end date.

    The return date is a full field in its own tinted box, not a footnote. The model is a course of care that gets re-measured, and the sheet should make that structural rather than implied.

  • How colour is allocated

    Blue carries structure: bands, section rules, document titles, and the NP's pen. Green carries the prescription, and nothing else. Warm greys are substrate: labels, descriptors, separators. Charcoal is body text. When a new element needs colour, ask which of those four jobs it does. If the answer is “none, it just needs to stand out”, it should not be coloured.

Type

Avenir throughout. Didot is reserved for a single accent word on patient-facing and editorial pieces, and is kept off clinical forms entirely, where its contrast hurts legibility at the sizes these documents run. Sizes are in points because everything here is print-first.

  • Why the labels are so small

    Field labels run at 5.8pt with heavy tracking. They are legible at arm's length but never compete with the content they name. The tracking is what keeps them readable at that size, and it is the single most characteristic typographic move in the system.

Structural devices

Six elements do nearly all the work. Reuse them before inventing anything.

FOUNDATIONAL LABS

Full-bleed blue band

Marks the non-negotiable. Used once per document, for the foundation only. Breaks the page margin with a negative margin so it runs edge to edge.

Specific item / formulation

Tiny caps label

5.8pt, 600 weight, .18em tracking, warm grey. Names a field or a section without competing with content. The most repeated element in the system.

Ruled slot

A write-on field. Bottom rule only, in the heavier rule grey so it survives a pen.

Filled disc / open ring

The marking device. A filled green disc reads as a stamp, not a tick. Deliberately not a square checkbox, because checkboxes imply a menu.

Return for reassessment

Green-tint box

Reserved for the return date. The one moment of green field on the sheet.

Pitfalls, learned the hard way

Every one of these caused a real bug in this project. Check them before shipping anything new.

  • Backgrounds vanish in print.

    The blue band, the green box and the charcoal slabs are CSS backgrounds, which browsers drop to white by default. print-color-adjust: exact is forced in the print CSS. Printing from a browser dialog instead needs “Background graphics” ticked.

  • Never verify page fit with scrollHeight.

    With margin-top: auto in a flex column, content overflows without growing scrollHeight, so the check reports zero while a row hangs off the page. Measure each element's bottom against the sheet's bottom instead. This bug shipped once and cut the last pathway off every sheet.

  • Line-height inherits into embedded sheets.

    The sheets are authored against the browser default. Any page that embeds them and sets a body line-height inflates every text block and overflows the page. Pin line-height: normal on the sheet when embedding.

  • Inlined logo classes collide.

    The supplied SVG carries its colours as .cls-1 / .cls-2 in a style block. Inlined more than once per page, those definitions compete. The fills are baked onto the paths in assets/wi-logo-horizontal.svg.

  • The logo grey is not the palette grey.

    The wordmark is a flat #808081, cooler than the palette's warm #918A82. Do not recolour the logo to match the palette, and do not sample the logo for text colour.

Adding something new

A short checklist that has caught most problems before they reached paper.

  • 1. Does it read as a menu?

    If a patient could interpret the new element as a choice they make, redesign it. This is the only question that can fail a design outright.

  • 2. Does it reuse a device?

    Tiny caps label, ruled slot, tinted box, disc, dashed row. Reach for these before drawing something new. Novelty is the enemy of a house style.

  • 3. Is the colour doing one of the four jobs?

    Structure, prescription, substrate, body text. If not, it should not be coloured.

  • 4. Does it fit the page, measured properly?

    Compare each element's bottom against the sheet's bottom. Do not trust scrollHeight.

  • 5. Does it survive print?

    Render to PDF and look at it. Backgrounds, hairlines under 0.5pt, and anything relying on screen antialiasing are the usual casualties.

  • 6. Are the gaps visible?

    Missing data becomes a dashed placeholder row, never a silent omission. Someone should be able to see what is still owed.
